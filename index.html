Hi! I am Donghyun (Daniel) Choi, PhD Candidate in Operations Management at Georgia Institute of Technology, advised by André Calmon and Beril Toktay. My research investigates the operational implications of sustainability targets and provides managerial and policy insights using analytical modeling and empirical methods. Specifically, I am studying this in the context of packaging material recycling, corporate carbon targets, and energy system decarbonization. I was nominated as a Novelis Graduate Scholar and am closely working with companies in the Drawdown Georgia Business Compact. I am on the 2026–2027 academic job market.

Business and Climate Change

Firms are working to decarbonize their operations and supply chains, which exposes them to carbon-transition risk. This risk reflects uncertainty across three closely linked dimensions: technological progress, policy stringency, and timing. In this stream of research, I study the role of slack resources in firms’ commitments to reduce carbon emissions and how markets perceive the climate risk of committed firms.

Slack and Science-Based Target Commitments

Donghyun Choi, Abhinav Shubham, and Manpreet Hora

Presented at: ARCS (2026, Singapore); POMS (2025, Atlanta; 2024, Minneapolis); MSOM (2024, Minneapolis).

Abstract

The Science-Based Targets initiative (SBTi) has become the leading framework for externally validated corporate decarbonization targets, yet commitment varies markedly even among firms facing comparable stakeholder pressures. We examine how a firm's internal resource position shapes its likelihood of committing to the SBTi, distinguishing between unabsorbed slack (e.g., liquid, readily redeployable resources) and absorbed slack (e.g., resources embedded in operating routines and administrative structures). Using a discrete-time hazard model estimated by probit on a global panel of 13,465 firms from 2014 to 2021, we find that the two forms of slack have opposing associations with commitment. Unabsorbed slack is negatively associated with commitment, consistent with liquid resources enabling managers to postpone an uncertain undertaking, whereas absorbed slack is positively associated with commitment, consistent with embedded resources supplying the coordination capacity that target setting requires. The negative relationship between unabsorbed slack and commitment is stronger when Scope 3 emissions constitute a larger share of total emissions and weaker among firms with prior experience of reducing supply-chain emissions. The positive relationship between absorbed slack and commitment is stronger among firms with internal carbon pricing or climate-linked incentives. Our findings indicate that financial liquidity alone does not translate into credible climate undertakings such as committing to the SBTi. Firms should pair embedded organizational capacity with governance practices that direct it toward their climate objectives.

02

Material Circularity

Producers using packaging materials such as plastic, aluminum, and paper face an expanding landscape of packaging extended producer responsibility (P-EPR) policies. Policymakers use a mix of levers, including producer fees, requirements that producer responsibility organizations meet recycling targets, and recycled-content mandates for packaging. In this stream, I study how material ownership and the funding structure of recycling systems can be designed to coordinate stakeholders. I also explore the role of advanced recycling technologies in recycling networks.

Publications

Prior to my PhD studies, I studied energy-system transitions from technology and policy perspectives. I was part of a research team at the KAIST Center for Sustainable Development and involved in developing an electricity-sector model that can analyze the economic and environmental impacts of renewable-capacity expansion and demand-side policy interventions at the national level. I am interested in operations and supply chain challenges in an evolving electricity-market environment with AI-driven increases in energy consumption and geopolitical risk.

  1. Choi, D., and Kim, Y. J. (2023). “Local and global experience curves for lumpy and granular energy technologies.” Energy Policy, 174, 113426. Paper ↗

    Abstract

    This study compares how local and global learning affect the costs of lumpy and granular electricity-generation technologies in Korea. Using one- and two-factor experience curves, it identifies the drivers of technological learning and evaluates the relative importance of domestic and international experience. The findings indicate that rapidly declining costs make granular technologies important for climate mitigation, although further reductions require lower solar soft costs and greater wind deployment. Knowledge spillovers also appear faster and more frequent for granular technologies than for lumpy technologies, highlighting the value of national absorptive capacity and strong international innovation networks.

  2. Choi, D., Eom, J., and Cho, C. H. (2023). “Integrating Market and Technology Uncertainties into Levelized Costs of Power Generation Technologies: A Case of Korea.” Energy Strategy Reviews, 45, 101046. Paper ↗

    Abstract

    This study evaluates the technologies and policies needed for Korea's transition to a low-carbon power sector. It decomposes the historical costs of five major generation technologies into key components, identifies their drivers and variability, and projects their 2030 cost distributions using Monte Carlo simulation. The analysis finds that business-as-usual changes in technological, economic, and market conditions are unlikely to make the proposed transition economically viable. Carbon pricing or stringent environmental policy is therefore needed for renewable energy and lower-carbon gas generation to remain competitive with coal.

  3. Choi, D., Ahn, Y. H., and Choi, D. G. (2020). “Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis of Electricity Sector Transition Policy in Korea.” Energy Strategy Reviews, 29, 100485. Paper ↗

    Abstract

    This study links a multi-criteria decision-making model with an energy-system model to assess realistic electricity-transition scenarios in Korea. Although the government's transition policy performs poorly when evaluated only through total cost and emissions, it becomes more attractive when broader social and environmental criteria are considered. Trade-off analyses across the criteria consistently favor pathways that reduce coal generation, illustrating how integrated assessment can clarify otherwise conflicting sustainability priorities.

  4. Kim, J. T., Cho, C. H., and Choi, D. (2019). “A CGE Analysis of Peak Load Pricing using Electricity Sector Detailed Database.” Korean Energy Economic Review, 18(2), 1–37. Paper ↗

    Abstract

    This study develops a CGE model that disaggregates the electricity sector by load-generation technology pairs using the methodology of GTAP9 Power DB and analyze the impact of peak-load rate increase. The Power DB is updated with Korea electricity market data and used as model input data. Increase in peak-load rate reduces the output of the electricity sector and electricity-intensive manufacturing sectors. Although total electricity consumption is reduced by the rate increase, load shift from peak to base increases the share of coal and nuclear in generation portfolio. Sensitivity analysis on substitution elasticity between loads, a key parameter, shows that more flexible load shift to less cost base-load increases the total electricity consumption and the output in the manufacturing sector. In the meanwhile higher base-load demand utilizes more coal power generation which exacerbates the carbon mitigation effect of peak-load rate increase.

  5. Choi, D., Kasdan, D. O., and Yoon, D. K. (2019). “Analyzing Disaster Loss Trends: A Comparison of Normalization Methodologies in South Korea.” Risk Analysis, 39(4), 859–870. Paper ↗

    Abstract

    This study evaluates how alternative normalization methods affect estimates of long-term disaster-loss trends. Eight formulas incorporating inflation, wealth, and societal factors are applied to 50 years of South Korean disaster data. Depending on the selected method, normalized losses exhibit either increasing or decreasing trends, and some results contain biases associated with Korea's distinctive development trajectory. The findings demonstrate why normalization choices matter and offer lessons for selecting suitable methods in other national contexts.
